What MishMash is, in five questions: why, what, who, where, when — and end with how you can take part. MishMash is one of the national AI research centres funded by the Research Council of Norway. We study AI from the perspective of creativity.

Why?

First question: why does a centre like this exist at all.
Headlines from this year. Threat on one side: actors and musicians campaigning against training on their work, church musicians reacting to AI songs at funerals, UNESCO reporting falling income for artists. Promise on the other: transformed creativity, reinvented film production, productivity gains. Both are true at once, and that confusion is where the centre gets its name.

Threat

  • Models trained on artists' work without agreement
  • Falling income across the creative sector
  • A narrower, more uniform culture
  • Energy, water and hardware

Promise

  • New instruments, new forms, new works
  • Archives that can finally be searched
  • Tools in the hands of people without a studio
  • Methods that travel out of the arts
You can find good evidence for either column. The mistake is to treat the question as settled in either direction. Our job is to produce evidence about specific systems in specific uses, rather than opinions about AI in general. Most public arguments pick a side. A research centre has to hold both.

mish·mash

noun — a confused mixture.

People always ask about the name. It is not an acronym. It describes the deliberate mix of artistic and scientific disciplines — and the fact that we are merging them into something new. The full story is on arj.no and in the FAQ on mishmash.no. Not an acronym. It began as a working title describing how the founders saw the group that had assembled. Among more than 150 centre proposals pitched to the Research Council in 2024, it was one of the few names people remembered. So it stuck.
Two circles, apart. Most attention goes to what the machines can do — benchmarks, capabilities, model releases. That is one circle. People get discussed as users, or as the ones being replaced. That is the other.
And together. All technology, AI included, is made by people and for people, so the interesting things happen where the circles overlap. That is where MishMash lives: the meeting point between human and machine, and between art and science.

Definitions

AI
The science and engineering of making intelligent machines. McCarthy 1955
Creativity
The ability to form novel and meaningful ideas or works. Boden 2004
Creative AI
Machine systems that produce results that are novel and meaningful — not random, and not copies. de Vries 2020
CoCreative AI
Systems built for genuine partnership, where human and machine contribute to the result. Anscomb 2024
Four words we lean on. McCarthy's definition is deliberately broad: he coined the term in the 1955 Dartmouth proposal, and it covers rule-based and search-based systems just as much as today's learning ones. Worth saying out loud, because most people now hear "AI" and think only of large language models. We have had AI since the 1950s; what changed is which kind is in the news. If a legal definition is wanted instead, the EU AI Act (Art. 3(1)) defines an AI system as a machine-based system designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy, that may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment, and that infers from its input how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions. Check the exact wording before quoting it verbatim. The other three are in the same wording as the glossary on mishmash.no. "Novel and meaningful" is a demanding pair: a random generator is novel and not meaningful; a copy is meaningful and not novel. The open question is not whether a machine can be creative in the abstract, but how human and machine creativity differ — and that is what CoCreative AI is about: partnership rather than replacement. Creativity is a fundamental human trait. It has shaped technology, and been shaped by it, for as long as there has been technology.

Why start with the arts

They push harder

Artistic use breaks a system in ways benchmark tests never reach.

They ask first

Authorship, consent and ownership arrived in the arts before they arrived anywhere else.

They show the public

A performance or an exhibition puts the question in front of people who will never read a paper.

This is the strategic bet of the centre. Not "AI applied to art", but art as the method for interrogating AI. If you work on AI anywhere else, the creative sector is a good place to look for the failure modes, and for the methods that keep people in control. Artists have been putting these systems under pressure since the 1950s — they find the edges first. Artistic exploration is our entry point for critical discussion about AI.

What?

Second question: what the centre actually does.

MishMash will
create, explore and reflect on AI
for, through and in creative practices.

The whole centre in one sentence. Three verbs and three prepositions, and the prepositions matter. Create: build systems, tools, artworks, frameworks and policies. Explore: use AI in creative practice, and take creative methods into other domains. Reflect: study the impacts critically. AI for creative practices, AI through creative practices, AI in creative practices.

Seven themes, one work package each. Some use AI in artistic applications; others take creative methods into adjacent domains — health, education, heritage, problem-solving. Each WP has a lead and two sidekicks from different institutions, deliberately.

Create · explore · reflect

CREATE

Making AI-based systems, tools and artworks — and the frameworks and policies around them.

EXPLORE

Using AI in creative practice, and taking creative methods into other fields.

REFLECT

Critically studying what AI does to people, culture and society.

These three are also three kinds of people: engineers and artists create; educators, clinicians and curators explore; scholars from sociology, philosophy and law reflect. Usually each group works at its own level, alone. Putting them in the same room is most of what the centre is for. Every work package does all three. It is also not a bad description of what happens in any lab.

Strings On-Line — self-playing guitars that sense the room. NIME 2020.

Acoustic guitars with microcomputers, sensors and actuators, so generated sound radiates from a wooden body rather than a loudspeaker. The input side is the point: the guitars sense people moving in the space, so the mapping from human action to sound is the design problem. Every system most people have met is disembodied — you type, a file comes back. A body changes what a system can know, and what it can be held to.

Machines · humans · society

Machines

What can these systems actually do, and why? Data, models, limits.

Humans

How do we create with them? Agency, skill, bodies, craft.

Society

What changes when they enter the world? Authorship, law, economy, education.

Many disagreements about AI are really disagreements about which of these three you are talking about. A claim about what a model can do is not a claim about what it does to a profession.

What it looks like

One picture per work package, from the people actually doing the work. Pick two or three to talk about depending on the room — for a general audience WP4 and WP6 usually land best.

WP1 — AI for artistic performances

Live music and art where people and machines improvise together.

Kyrre Glette (UiO) · Ivar Grydeland (NMH) · Georgios Marentakis (HiØ)

Real-time interaction with AI in performance, installations and games, with the emphasis on multiple and embodied agents. It deliberately counters the trend of very large models with hard-to-control outputs, balancing data-based approaches with artists’ knowledge and search-based methods. The open question: what has to be explainable while you are actually playing?

WP2 — AI in artistic processes

How AI changes the making of film, games, music and visual art.

Budhaditya Chattopadhyay (UiB) · Sashi Komandur (INN) · Synne Tollerud Bull (Kristiania)

Prompt-based systems are becoming normal in production workflows. Two worries: outsourcing creative decisions, and the cultural biases baked into commercial tools. When systems reinforce dominant patterns they narrow what gets made — so the question is one of cultural agency: who decides what stories are told and what images are seen?

WP3 — Creative use of AI for health and well-being

Arts therapies, emotional expression, and what AI does to well-being.

Claire Ghetti (UiB) · Andreas Bergsland (NTNU) · Jonna Vuoskoski (UiO)

Engaging in creative processes is good for health — that evidence is solid. What AI-generated content does to that is not. The WP studies both how people relate to AI-made work and how AI can support arts therapies, with consent, equity and critical disability perspectives at the centre rather than bolted on.

WP4 — Creative use of AI in education

AI literacy for schools, higher education and lifelong learning.

Eirik Sørbø (UiA) · Sidsel Karlsen (NMH) · Fredrik Graver (INN)

The sector is full of local initiatives adopting commercial tools, while high-quality open resources on creative AI are scarce — and hardly any exist in Norwegian. The aim is to equip future teachers and to close the generational gap in AI literacy.

WP5 — AI in the creative and cultural industries

Copyright, business models, labour and sustainability.

Ragnhild Brøvig (UiO) · Irina Eidsvold-Toien (BI) · Jon Marius Aareskjold-Drecker (UiT)

Four interlocking areas: law, rights and governance; economy, industry and labour; artistic practice and audiences; ethics, diversity and sustainability. Copyright and identity misuse, fair value distribution, and a life-cycle analysis of the environmental cost.

WP6 — AI for cultural heritage

Opening up archives, libraries and museums.

Ingrid Romarheim Haugen (NB) · Arnulf Mattes (UiB) · Olivier Lartillot (UiO)

The ABM sector — archives, libraries, museums — with the National Library and its Digital Humanities Lab. Hybrid models blending machine learning with musicology and symbolic AI, and particular attention to minority expressions: Norwegian folk music, Sámi joik, world chant cultures. The public sector has to lead here so the capability is used ethically.

WP7 — Human-centric AI for creative problem-solving

From industrial design to emergency response.

Carsten Griwodz (UiO) · Baltasar Beferull-Lozano (SimulaMet) · Kjetil Nordby (AHO)

Creative AI for people with a goal and a deadline: designers, filmmakers, industrial operators, emergency responders. These systems work under stricter boundaries than the others — physics, law, realism constraints — and the WP is about encoding those limits into the design principles rather than working around them.

Scholars from sociology, philosophy and law. They ask what it means, and what changes.

At RITMO we have had music researchers, psychologists and informatics people in the same building for nine years, sharing a coffee machine. That is what made the interdisciplinary work real rather than nominal. MishMash is distributed, so we have to manufacture that proximity — which is what the weekly and biweekly rhythm is for. Usually these three groups work at their own level, alone. Co-location is what changes that.

Intra, cross, multi, inter, trans. A centre needs all of them at different moments. Jensenius, Sound Actions (MIT Press, 2022)

Do you work interdisciplinary, or are you interdisciplinary? Most so-called interdisciplinary projects are cross-disciplinary: everyone stays comfortable in their own discipline and contributes a piece. The real thing starts when the terminology breaks down and people step on each other's toes. It is painful, and it is where the new things come from.

Ten definitions of noise

Randomness

the mathematician

A fluctuation

the physicist

The unwanted part

signal processing

Material

the sound artist

Disturbance

the philosopher

The signal

whoever is recording ventilation this summer

And it changes with what you are doing. I spent a summer recording ventilation systems for a project, so for me the ventilation was the signal, not the noise. The lesson: do not resolve the disagreement too early. The friction is the content. At a MishMash workshop, ten people from different backgrounds each gave a ten-minute talk on one word: noise. Ten definitions, no agreement, and a very good discussion — that is what multidisciplinary looks like in practice.

Declared, not hidden

An AI colophon says where AI contributes, so nobody has to guess. mishmash.no/about/ai-colophon/

A slide for the question that always comes: how did you make this? The website is not a brochure, it is part of the research — a way to create, explore and reflect on the tools we study. It lives in git like any other research output, and so does this presentation: same organisation, a repository each. How the work actually goes: the sources go in a folder — the invitation, the centre application, the previous deck, the website content — with a paragraph of instructions. The agent reads them, drafts the slides and the speaker notes, and keeps a log. I review and give short corrections; it fixes and updates the log. A conversation rather than an order. Three commitments from the colophon. Humans are responsible: output is reviewed before it goes live, and errors are ours, not the machine's. Nothing is hidden: AI involvement is declared on the colophon page, marked on translated pages, and visible in the commit history through Co-Authored-By trailers. And it is an experiment — we want to try things, test things, and say so when they break.
