What MishMash is, in five questions: why, what, who, where, when — and end with how you can take part. MishMash is one of the national AI research centres funded by the Research Council of Norway. We study AI from the perspective of creativity.

Why?

First question: why does a centre like this exist at all.
Headlines from this year. Threat on one side: actors and musicians campaigning against training on their work, church musicians reacting to AI songs at funerals, UNESCO reporting falling income for artists. Promise on the other: transformed creativity, reinvented film production, productivity gains. Both are true at once, and that confusion is where the centre gets its name.

Threat

  • Control slipping from maker to system
  • Training on artists’ work without agreement
  • A narrower, more uniform culture
  • Energy, water and hardware

Promise

  • Machines as partners, not just tools
  • New instruments, new forms, new works
  • Archives that can finally be searched
  • Methods that travel out of the arts
The pairs are taken from the centre application's seven challenges, each of which sets an opportunity against a constraint. Control is the one it repeats most — maintaining control, human agency, control and expression — and co-creation is its central positive claim: CoCreative AI, partnership rather than replacement. You can find good evidence for either column. The mistake is to treat the question as settled in either direction. Our job is to produce evidence about specific systems in specific uses, rather than opinions about AI in general. Most public arguments pick a side. A research centre has to hold both.
Two circles, apart. Most attention goes to what the machines can do — benchmarks, capabilities, model releases. That is one circle. People get discussed as users, or as the ones being replaced. That is the other.
And together. All technology, AI included, is made by people and for people, so the interesting things happen where the circles overlap. That is where MishMash lives: the meeting point between human and machine, and between art and science.
And there it is. Humans and machines overlap; science and art overlap; MishMash is where they meet. It is the centre's own emblem, and worth reading as an argument rather than a decoration: we are not the study of machines, and not the study of art. We are what happens in the intersection.

mish·mash

noun — a confused mixture.

People always ask about the name. It is not an acronym. It describes the deliberate mix of artistic and scientific disciplines — and the fact that we are merging them into something new. The full story is on arj.no and in the FAQ on mishmash.no. Not an acronym. It began as a working title describing how the founders saw the group that had assembled. Among more than 150 centre proposals pitched to the Research Council in 2024, it was one of the few names people remembered. So it stuck.

Why start with the arts

They push harder

Artistic use breaks a system in ways benchmark tests never reach.

They ask first

Authorship, consent and ownership arrived in the arts before they arrived anywhere else.

They show the public

A performance or an exhibition puts the question in front of people who will never read a paper.

This is the strategic bet of the centre. Not "AI applied to art", but art as the method for interrogating AI. If you work on AI anywhere else, the creative sector is a good place to look for the failure modes, and for the methods that keep people in control. Artists have been putting these systems under pressure since the 1950s — they find the edges first. Artistic exploration is our entry point for critical discussion about AI.

What?

Second question: what the centre actually does.

MishMash will createexplorereflect on AI forthroughin creative practices.

The whole centre in one sentence. Three verbs and three prepositions, and the prepositions matter. Create: build systems, tools, artworks, frameworks and policies. Explore: use AI in creative practice, and take creative methods into other domains. Reflect: study the impacts critically. AI for creative practices, AI through creative practices, AI in creative practices. Create means making AI-based systems, tools and artworks, and the frameworks and policies around them. Explore means using AI in creative practice, and taking creative methods into other fields. Reflect means critically studying what AI does to people, culture and society. Every work package does all three. It is also not a bad description of what happens in any lab.

The research question

What are the possibilities, limitations, and transformative effects of AI on creative practices, and how can we develop CoCreative AI systems that complement human creativity while addressing ethical, cultural, and societal implications?

One question, from the centre application, and the wording on mishmash.no. The first half is descriptive — what AI does to creative practice. The second half is constructive — what we should build instead. And the last clause is why the centre needs the humanities and law as much as it needs engineering. The three perspectives — machines, humans, society — are how we cut it.

Definitions

AI
The science and engineering of making intelligent machines. McCarthy 1955
Creativity
The ability to form novel and meaningful ideas or works. Boden 2004
Creative AI
Machine systems that produce results that are novel and meaningful — not random, and not copies. de Vries 2020
CoCreative AI
Systems built for genuine partnership, where human and machine contribute to the result. Anscomb 2024
Five words we lean on, in a ladder: what intelligence is, what the science of building it is, what creativity is, and the two kinds of creative machine. Legg and Hutter arrived at theirs by collecting some seventy definitions from psychology and AI and distilling what they had in common, which is why it is worth quoting rather than inventing one. Note what it does not say: nothing about brains, nothing about humans. It applies to a person, an animal and a program alike, and “a wide range of environments” is the demanding part — a system that is superb at exactly one thing is not intelligent by this measure. If a human-centred definition is wanted instead, the 1997 Mainstream Science on Intelligence statement, signed by 52 researchers, calls it a very general mental capability involving reasoning, planning, problem-solving, abstract thinking, comprehension of complex ideas, and learning from experience. McCarthy's definition is deliberately broad: he coined the term in the 1955 Dartmouth proposal, and it covers rule-based and search-based systems just as much as today's learning ones. Worth saying out loud, because most people now hear "AI" and think only of large language models. We have had AI since the 1950s; what changed is which kind is in the news. If a legal definition is wanted instead, the EU AI Act (Art. 3(1)) defines an AI system as a machine-based system designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy, that may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment, and that infers from its input how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions. Check the exact wording before quoting it verbatim. The other three are in the same wording as the glossary on mishmash.no. "Novel and meaningful" is a demanding pair: a random generator is novel and not meaningful; a copy is meaningful and not novel. The open question is not whether a machine can be creative in the abstract, but how human and machine creativity differ — and that is what CoCreative AI is about: partnership rather than replacement. Creativity is a fundamental human trait. It has shaped technology, and been shaped by it, for as long as there has been technology.

How?

How the work is actually organised, and what each of the seven work packages does.
To explain how a collaboration this large works, we made a cube. Start with it empty and add one face at a time. The front face: the seven themes, one work package each. This is where the disciplines and the partner institutions live. The top face: the three approaches. Create means building systems and works; explore means trying them out in performances, classrooms, clinics and museums; reflect means the ethical, legal, cultural and societal questions. Every work package does all three. The side face: the three perspectives. Machines — what can these systems actually do, and why? Data, models, limits. Humans — how do we create with them? Agency, skill, bodies, craft. Society — what changes when they enter the world? Authorship, law, economy, education. Many disagreements about AI are really disagreements about which of the three you are talking about. Cross-cutting workshops are organised along these two axes, so the work packages are not silos. A structured mesh, built out of the mishmash.

WP1 — AI for artistic performances

Live music and art where people and machines improvise together.

Kyrre Glette (UiO) · Ivar Grydeland (NMH) · Georgios Marentakis (HiØ)

Real-time interaction with AI in performance, installations and games, with the emphasis on multiple and embodied agents. It deliberately counters the trend of very large models with hard-to-control outputs, balancing data-based approaches with artists’ knowledge and search-based methods. The open question: what has to be explainable while you are actually playing?

WP2 — AI in artistic processes

How AI changes the making of film, games, music and visual art.

Budhaditya Chattopadhyay (UiB) · Sashi Komandur (INN) · Synne Tollerud Bull (Kristiania)

Prompt-based systems are becoming normal in production workflows. Two worries: outsourcing creative decisions, and the cultural biases baked into commercial tools. When systems reinforce dominant patterns they narrow what gets made — so the question is one of cultural agency: who decides what stories are told and what images are seen?

WP3 — Creative use of AI for health and well-being

Arts therapies, emotional expression, and what AI does to well-being.

Claire Ghetti (UiB) · Andreas Bergsland (NTNU) · Jonna Vuoskoski (UiO)

Engaging in creative processes is good for health — that evidence is solid. What AI-generated content does to that is not. The WP studies both how people relate to AI-made work and how AI can support arts therapies, with consent, equity and critical disability perspectives at the centre rather than bolted on.

WP4 — Creative use of AI in education

AI literacy for schools, higher education and lifelong learning.

Eirik Sørbø (UiA) · Sidsel Karlsen (NMH) · Fredrik Graver (INN)

The sector is full of local initiatives adopting commercial tools, while high-quality open resources on creative AI are scarce — and hardly any exist in Norwegian. The aim is to equip future teachers and to close the generational gap in AI literacy.

WP5 — AI in the creative and cultural industries

Copyright, business models, labour and sustainability.

Ragnhild Brøvig (UiO) · Irina Eidsvold-Toien (BI) · Jon Marius Aareskjold-Drecker (UiT)

Four interlocking areas: law, rights and governance; economy, industry and labour; artistic practice and audiences; ethics, diversity and sustainability. Copyright and identity misuse, fair value distribution, and a life-cycle analysis of the environmental cost.

WP6 — AI for cultural heritage

Opening up archives, libraries and museums.

Ingrid Romarheim Haugen (NB) · Arnulf Mattes (UiB) · Olivier Lartillot (UiO)

The ABM sector — archives, libraries, museums — with the National Library and its Digital Humanities Lab. Hybrid models blending machine learning with musicology and symbolic AI, and particular attention to minority expressions: Norwegian folk music, Sámi joik, world chant cultures. The public sector has to lead here so the capability is used ethically.

WP7 — Human-centric AI for creative problem-solving

From industrial design to emergency response.

Carsten Griwodz (UiO) · Baltasar Beferull-Lozano (SimulaMet) · Kjetil Nordby (AHO)

Creative AI for people with a goal and a deadline: designers, filmmakers, industrial operators, emergency responders. These systems work under stricter boundaries than the others — physics, law, realism constraints — and the WP is about encoding those limits into the design principles rather than working around them.

Three verbs, and three kinds of people. Create: engineers and artists build the systems and make the work. Explore: educators, clinicians and curators put that work in front of people. Reflect: scholars from sociology, philosophy and law ask what it means and what changes. It is a cycle rather than a pipeline. What the reflecting turns up goes back into what gets built — that arrow round the bottom left is the one that usually does not exist. Usually these three groups work at their own level, alone. Co-location is what changes that: at RITMO we have had music researchers, psychologists and informatics people in the same building for nine years, sharing a coffee machine, and that is what made the interdisciplinary work real rather than nominal. MishMash is distributed, so the rhythm has to manufacture that proximity.

Jensenius, Sound Actions (MIT Press, 2022)

Five kinds of disciplinarity, built in the order that makes the argument rather than left to right. Inter first, because it is the word everyone uses. Then multi: side by side, each contributing from their own discipline. Then cross: borrowing a method but staying home. Then intra: depth in one field, which is where most of us actually live. And trans last: something that belongs to no existing discipline, with the arrow running all the way back to feed the individual disciplines again. Do you work interdisciplinary, or are you interdisciplinary? Most so-called interdisciplinary projects are cross-disciplinary: everyone stays comfortable in their own discipline and contributes a piece. The real thing starts when the terminology breaks down and people step on each other's toes. It is painful, and it is where the new things come from. A centre needs all of them at different moments.
A map for when artists and scientists talk past each other. Start with the axis: artistic research at one end, scientific research at the other, and a continuum between them rather than two camps. First click: practice at either end — artistic practice beyond artistic research, professional practice beyond scientific research. That is where most people actually spend their working life. Second click: design and engineering, which pull upwards out of both rather than sitting anywhere along the line. Third click: what each position produces. Practice makes artworks or products. Research, artistic or scientific, produces the things in the middle — reflection, builds, code, data, publications. The overlap is large, and that is the point. Fourth click: where the boundaries sit. Practice at the ends is its own thing; the research in the middle is one shared region; design and engineering are a region of their own. Knowledge-making and art-making go hand in hand.

Solid lines are lines of responsibility; dashed ones advise. The Board governs and takes the strategic decisions — it is chaired from outside the host institution. The Council gives strategic oversight and holds the partners to their commitments. The Scientific Advisory Board is international, and evaluates. The Stakeholder Board speaks for the partner organisations. Management does the day-to-day coordination. The Work Package Leader Group is the scientific engine: each of the seven work packages has a lead and two sidekicks, deliberately from three different institutions, so collaboration is built into the structure rather than hoped for. The point of drawing it this way: no single institution controls the centre.

That is why the website is built as a hub pointing at the partner institutions rather than a large archive of its own — when the centre ends, the institutions keep the work. Come to a MeshUp

Online, Thursdays 12:00–12:30. A talk and a discussion. Open to everyone, no registration.

Sign up for one or more work packages. Free. Open to researchers at partner institutions and to the unaffiliated. Write to contact@mishmash.no.

Declared, not hidden

An AI colophon says where AI contributes, so nobody has to guess. mishmash.no/about/ai-colophon/

A slide for the question that always comes: how did you make this? The website is not a brochure, it is part of the research — a way to create, explore and reflect on the tools we study. It lives in git like any other research output, and so does this presentation: same organisation, a repository each. How the work actually goes: the sources go in a folder — the invitation, the centre application, the previous deck, the website content — with a paragraph of instructions. The agent reads them, drafts the slides and the speaker notes, and keeps a log. I review and give short corrections; it fixes and updates the log. A conversation rather than an order. Three commitments from the colophon. Humans are responsible: output is reviewed before it goes live, and errors are ours, not the machine's. Nothing is hidden: AI involvement is declared on the colophon page, marked on translated pages, and visible in the commit history through Co-Authored-By trailers. And it is an experiment — we want to try things, test things, and say so when they break.
